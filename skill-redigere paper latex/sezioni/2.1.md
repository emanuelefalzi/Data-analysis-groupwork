## DRAFT

Staging gives investors an abandonment option [[CIT-P:cornelli2003]]. VC-controlled ventures are more likely to have cash-flow and control rights contingent on observable milestones [[CIT-P:kaplan2003]]. Staging models predict screening through discontinuing ventures with poor early results [[CIT-P:dahiya2012]]. In our setting, investors can abandon after new information. On this reading, the gap between the first and the second VC round represents the time needed to produce investable news.

Under Bayesian learning, each period without success lowers investors' belief that a project is good [[CIT-P:bergemann2005]]. In software and services, VC rounds can serve as experiments that generate information [[CIT-P:kerrnanda2014, ewens2018]]. We argue that, on this reading, good projects are refinanced sooner, so a short gap reads as good news.

The leash view treats round length as a governance choice. Staging can address founder-investor agency problems [[CIT-P:neher1999]]. [[CIT-N:gompers1995]] links staging to monitoring and reports shorter durations in industries with less tangible assets or greater research and development intensity. Greater investor distance was associated with more rounds and shorter intervals between them [[CIT-P:tian2011]]. In our setting, we argue that a short gap may mark a venture that is harder to monitor and riskier, giving the competing prediction.

Evidence links outcomes to the number and size of rounds, rarely to time. In US biotechnology, [[CIT-N:hoenen2014]] finds that patent applications are associated with larger first-round funding but not second-round funding. In our setting, we know of no study relating the gap to exit while retaining one-round startups.

H1 states that, among two-round startups, a longer gap is associated with a smaller probability of exit within five years. For β1, the coefficient of R × ln(1 + months), this predicts β1 < 0. The competing leash view predicts β1 ≥ 0.
